# Legal screen of a business idea

The goal is not a legal opinion. It is to find, before the founder spends
money, whether the business model itself runs into a legal wall, and what the
cheapest reliable way to confirm that is. A legal blocker is often the risk that
kills a small business, and founders rarely see it because the idea "works"
technically and commercially.

## How to work

1. **Name the jurisdiction.** Law differs by country and often by region. Use
   the one the idea implies (where the founder and the customers are); if it is
   unclear, say which one you assume and that the answer may differ elsewhere.
2. **Describe the activity in legal terms, not the founder's terms.** "Group
   buying where friends chip in and share the profit" is collecting money from
   the public with a promised return. "Reselling tickets" is secondary ticketing.
   "Automatic penalty charges" is taking payments without a per-transaction
   authorisation. The legal category decides which rules apply.
3. **Sort each finding into one of three kinds**, because they lead to
   different verdicts:
   - *Illegal as described*: the core model is prohibited or needs a licence
     that a small founder cannot realistically get. Changing the model is the
     only fix.
   - *Legal with compliance work*: registrations, terms, consents, insurance,
     tax, labelling. Estimate the cost and time.
   - *Uncertain*: depends on facts not in the description or on how a regulator
     would classify it. Say which fact decides it.
4. **Put a number on compliance** where you can: order-of-magnitude cost and
   time before the first sale (a lawyer's review, a licence, insurance,
   certification). Say when the cost changes the unit economics.
5. **End with the questions for a lawyer**: the two or three facts that must be
   confirmed, and the cheapest way (a one-hour consultation with a specialist in
   that area, a regulator's published guidance, a trade association).

## Honesty rules

- State laws and thresholds only when you are confident they exist; otherwise
  describe the kind of rule ("many EU countries cap resale prices for event
  tickets") and mark it as to be verified. A wrong specific citation is worse
  than a correctly hedged general one.
- "Everyone does it" and "the platform allows it" are not legal defences:
  platforms enforce their own rules and regulators enforce the law
  independently.
- Founder claims of legal approval ("our lawyer said it's fine") are unverified;
  note what the approval would have to cover.
- Do not soften a clear prohibition into "may face legal challenges". If the
  core activity is prohibited as described, say so plainly.
